01

Turn a shape into a probability distribution

The target tells the sampler what counts as a plausible outcome.

Grey: component centres μⱼ, equally spaced along the contour. Blue: independent samples from p₀. The faint circle shows one component’s 2σ₀ radius.

Choose M = 64 points μ₁,…,μM along the selected outline. Give each a small Gaussian neighbourhood with σ₀ = 0.08. The resulting smooth density concentrates around a recognizable contour:

p₀(x) = 1M ∑j=1M 𝒩(x; μj, σ₀²I),   x ∈ ℝ².

To sample it directly, draw a uniform component index J and z ∼ 𝒩(0,I), then set x₀ = μJ + σ₀z. The walkthrough uses these known components to compute a score exactly.

What is being generated? Each dot is one 2D sample. Many samples trace the shape. This is a diffusion model of a contour distribution; an image diffusion model instead treats an entire pixel or latent array as one sample.

The target has nonzero thickness. A perfectly thin curve would be a singular distribution with no ordinary smooth density on ℝ² at t = 0.

02

Erase structure with a known noising process

Learning the reverse direction begins with a forward process we can sample exactly.

Signal a = 1.000Noise σ = 0.000a² + σ² = 1

This slider reuses the same x₀ and ε at each t to compare marginals smoothly. It is a coupling of noisy snapshots, not a Brownian sample path. The Diffusion lab has full forward OU paths.

Use the variance-preserving Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

dXt = −½Xtdt + dWt.

Its transition from a clean point is Gaussian. An integrating factor gives Xt = e−t/2X₀ + ∫₀ᵗe−(t−r)/2dWr; the integral has covariance (1−e−t)I. Thus each noisy marginal can be sampled as

xt = a(t)x₀ + σ(t)ε,   ε ∼ 𝒩(0,I),
a(t) = e−t/2,   σ(t)² = 1−e−t.

The signal shrinks while the Gaussian perturbation grows. Our Gaussian mixture stays analytically tractable:

pt(x) = 1/M ∑j 𝒩(x; aμj, vI),
v(t) = a²σ₀² + σ².

Keep the two variances separate: σ² is added noise conditional on x₀; v also includes the clean component’s thickness. As t → ∞, a → 0, σ → 1, and pt approaches 𝒩(0,I).

03

Use the score to read the noisy distribution

A noisy observation is ambiguous. The score combines all plausible clean explanations.

Noisy density pₜScore directionsProbe xₜConditional clean mean

Drag or click the plot; arrow keys move the probe. Shading is density divided by its maximum in this view. Arrow lengths are compressed. Grey links show the four strongest component responsibilities.

The score is the spatial gradient of log density:

st(x) = ∇x log pt(x).

For this mixture, Bayes’ rule gives a responsibility for each component. Differentiating the log of the mixture yields

wj(x,t) = exp(−‖x−aμj‖² / 2v)∑ℓ exp(−‖x−aμℓ‖² / 2v),
st(x) = [a∑jwjμj − x] / v.

The score pulls toward a weighted mean of plausible noisy components. At large t these overlap almost completely; at small t the weights become localized near the contour.

Why noise prediction learns this vector

∇x log q(x|x₀) = −(x−ax₀)/σ² = −ε/σ,
st(x) = 𝔼[−ε/σ | Xt = x].

A network trained with 𝔼‖ε−εθ(xt,t)‖² learns the conditional mean ε* = 𝔼[ε|xt] at the population optimum. Hence sθ ≈ −εθ/σ. This expectation, rather than the particular hidden noise realization, is what the squared-error objective identifies.

x̂₀(x,t) = 𝔼[X₀|x] = [x + σ²st(x)] / a.

The purple marker is this conditional clean mean. It can fall between modes, away from the contour. A one-shot conditional mean is generally not a sample from p₀; generation updates the noisy state over many noise levels. These identities use t > 0 and a > 0.

04

Take a small reverse step, then recompute

The sampler repeatedly asks for the score at the current location and current noise level.

In generation time u = T−t, the deterministic probability-flow velocity and stochastic reverse process are

bODE(x,t) = ½x + ½st(x),
dYu = [½Yu + st(Yu)]du + dBu.

The factor ½ on the ODE score is essential. The reverse SDE uses a full score and injects new noise; removing its noise without changing the drift gives the wrong density evolution.

What the numerical solver does

ODE predictor: x̃ = x + h bODE(x,t),
ODE corrector: x′ = x + ½h[bODE(x,t) + bODE(x̃,t−h)],
SDE update: x′ = x + h[½x+st(x)] + √h ξ,
ξ ∼ 𝒩(0,I),   h = t−t′ > 0.

The ODE uses Heun’s method; the SDE uses Euler–Maruyama. The quadratic grid tk = 12(1−k/480)² spends smaller steps near the narrow final density. Switch the dynamics above to see the different updates.

05

Understand why moving particles transports probability

The reverse process is justified at the level of densities, not by a visual resemblance.

The forward OU process satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation:

∂tp = ½∇·(xp) + ½Δp.

Since p st = ∇p, this can be written as a continuity equation

∂tp + ∇·(p vt) = 0,
vt(x) = −½x − ½st(x).

Changing clocks reverses the velocity: bODE = −vt. Write ρu = pT−u; then ∂uρ + ∇·(ρbODE) = 0. If Φu is the ODE solution map,

(Φu)#pT = pT−u,
pT−u(Φu(z)) |det DΦu(z)| = pT(z).

This is the mathematical meaning of transport: the flow changes locations and local volume while preserving total probability. Along a path, d log ρu(Yu)/du = −∇·bODE.

Why the reverse SDE has the same marginals

Its drift is ½x+st, and its Brownian term adds ½Δρ. Substituting ρ = pt gives

−∇·[(½x+st)ρ] + ½Δρ
= −½∇·(xρ) − ½Δρ = −∂tpt.

Thus the exact SDE and ODE evolve the same laws from pT. Their individual paths differ; neither reconstructs the particular forward Brownian path.

Where Wasserstein geometry enters

The forward density is a W₂ gradient flow of

ℱ(ρ) = ¼∫ ‖x‖²ρ dx + ½∫ ρ log ρ dx,
∂tρ = ∇·(ρ∇[δℱ/δρ]).

It decreases ½KL(ρ‖𝒩(0,I)), up to a constant. Running the known density curve backward recovers structure. This generative flow is not generally a W₂ shortest path. The Wasserstein lab shows displacement interpolation and JKO steps separately.

Exact statements, Gaussian initialization, and numerical error

For this finite Gaussian mixture with σ₀ > 0, densities and scores are smooth on the finite time interval. The pushforward and matching-marginal identities assume exact scores, initialization from pT, and exact integration. The application uses a finite solver and finite samples.

The default instead starts from an exact standard Gaussian. At finite T = 12, pT has a tiny remaining trace of the data distribution. Coupling XT = aX₀ + σZ with Z ∼ 𝒩(0,I), independent of X₀, gives

W₂(pT, 𝒩(0,I))² ≤ a²𝔼‖X₀‖² + 2(σ−1)².

This bounds the initial discrepancy, not its amplification by the reverse solver. “Exact noisy marginal” starts from the analytic p₁₂ and removes this discrepancy. In practical diffusion models, a learned score, a finite noise schedule, and numerical integration each contribute approximation error.

A standard discrete reverse mean

Let αk = 1−βk and ᾱk = ∏j≤kαj. A common DDPM parameterization uses

μθ(xk,k) = 1√αk[xk − βk√(1−ᾱk) εθ(xk,k)],
xk−1 = μθ(xk,k) + σk,revz.

The reverse variance is a sampler choice. One common fixed choice is β̃k = βk(1−ᾱk−1)/(1−ᾱk); implementations omit the final random increment. This discrete formula is explained here, while the live demo implements the continuous-time OU model.

What has actually been learned?

A trained network encodes a data prior through its denoising predictions. Noise alone does not contain a predetermined star, face, or scene. Here the target menu supplies that prior explicitly; in image models, training data and conditions determine it.

Repeated updates keep a sample compatible with a sequence of noisy distributions. Coarse organization becomes visible before fine details, but this is not simply subtracting a stored noise image or interpolating linearly to a chosen target.

This page runs the complete 2D sampling calculation locally. It contains no pretrained image network. The exact mixture makes the mechanism visible without hiding the score inside a neural model.

What is the model actually computing?

Each target is an equally weighted mixture of 64 isotropic Gaussians with standard deviation σ₀ = 0.11. For a(t) = e−t/2 and v(t) = a(t)²σ₀² + 1 − a(t)²:

pt(x) = 1/M ∑j 𝒩(x; a(t)μj, v(t)I)
st(x) = [∑j wj(x,t)a(t)μj − x] / v(t)

The weights w are normalized Gaussian responsibilities. Reverse samplers start from exact p₆, which is close to, but not exactly, a standard Gaussian. There is no neural network or training in this exhibit.

One family of densities, two ways to run it backward

The exact reverse SDE and probability-flow ODE have the same one-time marginals when initialized consistently and integrated exactly. The SDE has random paths; the ODE has deterministic paths given its initial point. Finite steps and finite samples create visible differences. Neither process retraces the forward Brownian path.

Sampling is a balance of drift and noise

dX = −V′(X) dt + √(2τ) dB
V(x) = x⁴/4 − x²/2 + bx

The force −V′ pulls particles downhill. Noise lets them cross the barrier. For τ > 0, the invariant density is

π(x) = Z⁻¹ exp[−V(x)/τ].
Try it in the lecture

Set τ to 0.08 and start in the left well. Then try τ = 0.70. Why can a sampler have the correct stationary density yet mix slowly?

Reveal the explanation

At low temperature the barrier is large relative to τ. Crossings become rare, so a short run can remain trapped near its starting mode. A stationary law describes equilibrium, not the time needed to reach it. The double well is nonconvex, so the usual global strong-convexity contraction bound does not apply.

The score connection

∇ log π = −∇V / τ   ⇒   dX = τ ∇ log π(X) dt + √(2τ) dB.

Langevin sampling uses a fixed target score. Reverse diffusion uses a family of noisy scores st and a changing clock. They are related sampling ideas, with different dynamics and objectives.

Match equal quantiles

In one dimension, optimal quadratic transport pairs the same cumulative mass u in both distributions.

Qs(u) = (1−s)Q₀(u) + sQ₁(u)
W₂²(ρ₀,ρ₁) = ∫₀¹ |Q₁(u)−Q₀(u)|² du

The alternative crossfade is simply ρs = (1−s)ρ₀ + sρ₁. It changes density weights instead of tracking particles.

A Wasserstein geodesic is a shortest path between specified endpoints. A Langevin density follows a free-energy gradient flow. A reverse diffusion trajectory is not generally either one's geodesic.

A computable JKO step: free-energy descent in Wasserstein space

For a quadratic potential V(x) = x²/2 and τ = 0.4, start from 𝒩(m,σ²) with m = 2 and σ = 0.25. Each step minimizes transport cost plus free energy. Within this Gaussian family, the minimizer is explicit.

ρk+1 ∈ arg minρ { W₂²(ρ,ρk)/(2h) + ℱ(ρ) }
Step 0m 2.000σ 0.250

Free energy (constant omitted): 2.586

Equilibrium: m = 0, σ = √0.4 ≈ 0.632. The implicit scheme approaches the continuous OU gradient flow as h → 0.

Current ρEquilibrium
Derive the update by minimizing over m and σ

For 1D Gaussians, W₂² = (m−mk)² + (σ−σk)². Up to an additive constant, ℱ = (m²+σ²)/2 − τ log σ. Set both partial derivatives of the JKO objective to zero:

mk+1 = mk/(1+h)
σk+1 = [σk + √(σk² + 4hτ(1+h))] / [2(1+h)].

The positive root keeps σ > 0. Comparing the minimum with the admissible choice ρ = ρk shows ℱ(ρk+1) + W₂²(ρk+1,ρk)/(2h) ≤ ℱ(ρk): every exact step decreases free energy.

A mathematical spine for the lecture

Read each model at three levels: a particle process, the evolution of its density, and the objective or geometry behind that evolution.

DEFINITIONS · DERIVATIONS · REFERENCES
Notation: t is forward diffusion time; u = T − t is reverse time; τ is Langevin temperature; st is the score; s without a subscript is the interpolation parameter. Brownian motions B and W have covariance tI. All numerical exhibits are low-dimensional teaching models.

1 / Forward diffusion and denoising

A discrete variance-preserving chain gradually adds Gaussian noise. Let αk = 1−βk and ᾱk = ∏j≤k αj. Then

q(xk|x₀) = 𝒩(√ᾱkx₀, (1−ᾱk)I).

Sample k, x₀, and ε ∼ 𝒩(0,I); form xk = √ᾱkx₀ + √(1−ᾱk)ε. A common DDPM objective is

ℒsimple = 𝔼 ‖ε − εθ(xk,k)‖².

This noise-prediction loss is a simplified weighting of the variational training objective. At its population optimum, εθ estimates 𝔼[ε|xk], giving the marginal score

sθ(xk,k) ≈ −εθ(xk,k) / √(1−ᾱk).

Continuous-time exhibit: β(t)=1, ᾱ(t)=e⁻ᵗ. A single Gaussian perturbation gives the correct noisy marginal, but does not by itself specify a Brownian trajectory. [1]

2 / Reverse SDE and probability-flow ODE

For dXt = f(Xt,t)dt + g(t)dWt, define st = ∇ log pt. Assuming sufficient smoothness and positive densities, in the increasing reverse clock u = T−t:

dYu = [−f(Yu,t) + g(t)²st(Yu)]du
+ g(t)dBu.

The corresponding probability-flow ODE in that same clock is

dY/du = −f(Y,t) + ½g(t)²st(Y).

The half factor matters. Substituting stpt = ∇pt into the Fokker–Planck equation yields an equivalent continuity equation with velocity f − ½g²s in forward time.

Exact scores and consistent initialization give matching marginals, not identical paths. Approximate scores and time discretization introduce error. These formulas assume scalar, state-independent g. [2]

3 / From Langevin particles to density evolution

dX = −∇V(X)dt + √(2τ)dB
∂tρ = ∇·(ρ∇V) + τΔρ.

The second equation is Fokker–Planck: drift transports probability and Brownian noise spreads it. The probability flux is J = −ρ∇V − τ∇ρ. If Z = ∫e−V/τdx is finite, π = Z⁻¹e−V/τ has zero flux.

Euler–Maruyama gives the unadjusted Langevin update

Xn+1 = Xn − h∇V(Xn) + √(2τh)ξn.

Finite h generally biases the stationary law. The quartic example uses a small h for illustration, not a theorem of numerical convergence. Stability and mixing depend on the potential, step size, and initial law.

4 / Free energy as the organizing principle

ℱ(ρ) = ∫ Vρ dx + τ∫ ρ log ρ dx
ℱ(ρ) − ℱ(π) = τ KL(ρ ‖ π).

The first term favours low potential; the second favours spreading. With first variation δℱ/δρ = V + τ(logρ+1), the density equation becomes

∂tρ = ∇·(ρ∇[δℱ/δρ]).

For smooth positive solutions with adequate decay or no-flux boundaries, integration by parts gives

dℱ/dt = −∫ ρ |∇(V+τ logρ)|² dx ≤ 0.

This is the Wasserstein gradient-flow structure. Under a globally λ-strongly convex potential (λ>0), suitable solutions satisfy W₂(ρt,π) ≤ e−λtW₂(ρ₀,π). The double-well exhibit does not meet that convexity condition. [3]

5 / What geometry adds — and what it does not say

For probability measures with finite second moments, quadratic Wasserstein distance is the least root-mean-square cost of coupling their mass:

W₂²(μ,ν) = infγ∈Π(μ,ν) ∫ |x−y|² dγ(x,y).

The dynamic formulation describes the same cost through a continuity equation: minimize ∫₀¹∫ρ|v|² dx ds subject to ∂sρ + ∇·(ρv)=0 and fixed endpoints. It connects particle velocity to motion through distribution space.

JKO replaces a small Euclidean gradient step with a minimization involving W₂²/(2h) and ℱ. The Gaussian experiment above makes this construction directly computable. Its time-discrete iterates approximate a gradient-flow curve as the step shrinks.

A diffusion-model score is an ordinary spatial gradient of log density. “Wasserstein gradient” refers to a different object: variation of a functional over distributions. The language is connected, but the two gradients are not interchangeable.

The Ricci-flow connection is a viewpoint: study an evolving geometric object through a governing flow. Here that object is a probability distribution; the simulations do not compute curvature or implement Ricci flow. [4]

Checkpoints

Checkpoint: why is “denoising = reversing noise” incomplete?

A noisy point does not uniquely identify the original datum. Generative denoising uses a distributional prior to sample plausible structure. With an exact score, reverse dynamics reconstruct the evolving laws, not the particular forward noise realization. In a learned model, the prior is encoded approximately by a trained network.

Checkpoint: can a density crossfade and a W₂ geodesic have the same mean?

Yes. Both interpolate the endpoint means linearly when those means exist. A mean alone cannot describe the distribution. Translating a narrow Gaussian illustrates the distinction: transport keeps its width, while a midpoint crossfade can be bimodal.

Checkpoint: what is the equilibrium in the Gaussian JKO experiment?

For V(x)=x²/2 and τ=0.4, π is 𝒩(0,0.4). Thus m*=0 and σ*=√0.4. Setting these values in both update formulas leaves them unchanged. Notice that 0.4 is the variance, not the standard deviation.

Numerical methods & reproducibility

The diffusion lab uses 640 particles and a 64-component Gaussian mixture. Forward OU transitions are exact. Reverse SDE integration is Euler–Maruyama; the probability-flow ODE uses Heun's method. A quadratic reverse-time grid resolves the low-noise end. Time horizon T=6, 360 steps, 121 stored frames.

The Langevin lab has 800 particles, a fixed step h=0.005, and seed 81 on reset. Its quartic potential is not globally Lipschitz. If a nonfinite state or |x|>20 occurs, the simulation stops and reports instability; particles are not clipped to manufacture convergence. The target normalization uses numerical quadrature on [−6,6].

The transport lab uses midpoint quantiles, bisection of analytic Gaussian-mixture CDFs, and a small Gaussian display kernel. W₂ is computed from the unsmoothed quantiles. JKO statistics follow the closed-form Gaussian update.

The Noise → shape walkthrough uses 600 samples, a 64-component mixture with σ₀=0.08, horizon T=12, and 480 steps on a quadratic time grid. The default initialization is exactly N(0,I); exact p₁₂ initialization is optional. Heun and Euler–Maruyama implement the displayed ODE and SDE. The forward slider couples marginal samples with fixed noise; it is not a Brownian trajectory. The score probe evaluates the analytic density and posterior, with a rescaled density background for display. Seeds initialize a deterministic pseudorandom generator; Gaussian draws use Box–Muller. No network requests, model downloads, telemetry, or external scripts are needed.
